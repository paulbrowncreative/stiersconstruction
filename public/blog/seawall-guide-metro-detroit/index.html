The Complete Seawall Guide for Lake St. Clair and the Detroit River

How seawalls work, which materials to choose, when to repair or replace, and what permits to expect on Lake St. Clair and the Detroit River.

If you own canal-front or lakefront property in Metro Detroit, your seawall is doing quiet, constant work. It keeps your yard from sliding into the water, protects a home’s foundation and gives you a solid edge for docks and boat hoists. This guide explains how seawalls work, what they are made of, how they fail, and what to expect when it is time to repair or replace one.

Key Takeaways

  • A seawall holds soil in place against waves, current and ice. Most failures start behind or below the wall, not on the visible face.
  • Vinyl, steel, galvanized steel and concrete each suit different loads, depths and site access.
  • Drainage and anchoring (French drains, tie-rods, deadmen) matter as much as the wall itself.
  • Work on Great Lakes waters, including Lake St. Clair, generally needs an EGLE permit and often a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers permit.

What a Seawall Does

A seawall (also called a bulkhead or retaining wall along the water) is a vertical barrier that holds soil back and takes the beating from waves, boat wakes, moving water and ice. On lots along Lake St. Clair, the Detroit River and the canals that feed them, the wall is usually the only thing separating a lawn from open water.

The visible part is only a small piece. A working seawall has a wall face driven deep into the ground, a cap along the top, anchors that tie the wall back into the soil, and drainage that keeps water pressure from building behind it. Our seawall anatomy guide covers each part in detail.

Seawall Materials at a Glance

Many residential and marina seawalls are built from sheet piling: interlocking panels driven into the ground side by side. The main choices are:

  • Vinyl: lightweight, corrosion-proof and common on residential shorelines and canals with moderate loads.
  • Steel: strong under heavy loads and in deeper water, but it needs corrosion protection over its life.
  • Galvanized steel: steel with a zinc coating that slows corrosion in wet, marine conditions.
  • Concrete: massive and long-lived, but heavier to build and usually needs more equipment access.

Soil, water depth, what the wall has to carry and how equipment can reach the site decide which one fits. Our comparison, vinyl vs. steel vs. concrete seawalls, walks through the trade-offs.

How Seawalls Fail

Seawalls rarely fail overnight. They fail slowly, and the early signs are easy to miss:

  • Water pressure behind the wall. Rain and groundwater collect in the soil behind a wall. If it cannot drain, hydrostatic pressure pushes the wall outward. French drains relieve that pressure.
  • Corroded or failed anchors. Tie-rods and anchors hold the wall upright. When they corrode or pull loose, the wall starts to lean.
  • Loss of soil at the base or ends. Moving water can scour soil from below the wall or flank around its ends.
  • Cap and seam damage. Cracked caps and open seams let water and soil escape through the wall.
  • Ice and wave load. Michigan winters add ice pressure and freeze-thaw cycles on top of normal wave action.

Read more in 7 warning signs your seawall is failing.

Repair or Replace?

A sound wall with minor problems can often be repaired: sealing seams, patching or replacing the cap, adding splash guards, and installing or clearing French drains. Regular maintenance helps prevent bigger repairs later. A wall that has lost its footing, leans significantly or has failed anchors usually needs to be replaced. The dependable way to know is to have someone inspect it in person, ideally with photos of the wall, the ground behind it and any sinkholes.

Permits: Plan for Them Early

Seawall work at or below the ordinary high-water mark on Lake St. Clair generally needs a permit from Michigan’s Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy (EGLE) and often the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. Applications go through EGLE’s MiEnviro Portal, which forwards them to the Corps. Read our Michigan permit guide for seawalls, docks and dredging before you schedule work.

Planning Your Project

  1. Document the problem. Take photos of the wall face, the cap, the ground behind it and any settling.
  2. Note what has to be protected. Homes, patios, docks and hoists tied into the wall change the design.
  3. Ask about access. Equipment may work from land, from a barge or both, and that affects material choice and schedule.
  4. Ask about permits. Find out who applies, what the application needs and how long it usually takes for your site.
  5. Get a written quote that lists materials, drainage, anchors, cap, and cleanup.

Questions to Ask a Seawall Contractor

  • Who will be on site during the work?
  • Which wall material do you recommend for my soil and water depth, and why?
  • How will you handle drainage and anchoring?
  • Who handles permits, and what do you need from me?
  • What will you do about my dock, hoist or patio during the work?

How a Sheet-Pile Seawall Is Typically Installed

Every site is different, but a sheet-pile installation generally follows this sequence:

  1. Site review and design. The contractor looks at soil, water depth, the existing wall, what sits behind it and how equipment can reach the site.
  2. Permits. Applications go in before regulated work begins. See the permit guide.
  3. Preparation. Old wall sections may be removed, and obstructions, landscaping and sometimes structures near the edge are cleared or protected.
  4. Driving the sheets. Panels are set and driven into the ground one after another, often with an excavator-mounted vibratory hammer, working from land, a barge or both.
  5. Anchoring. Tie-rods are connected between the wall and buried anchors so the wall can resist soil and water pressure.
  6. Drainage and backfill. Drains are installed and material is placed and compacted behind the wall.
  7. Cap and finish. The cap goes on, and the site is graded and cleaned up.

Seawall Alternatives

A vertical wall is not the only way to protect a shoreline. Depending on the site and what regulators allow, options include:

  • Riprap or stone revetments: sloped armor of large stone that absorbs wave energy rather than reflecting it.
  • Concrete or block walls: massive structures built in place or from units.
  • Natural or soft shoreline approaches: vegetation and other methods that work with the shoreline. These generally suit lower-energy sites.

Canal-front lots with docks and boats, where you need a vertical edge and deep water at the wall, usually call for a vertical wall. Exposed shorelines may perform differently. Ask what the permit agencies will accept for your site.

What Affects the Cost of a Seawall Project

We quote each project individually, since prices depend on many things. The main drivers are:

  • Length and height of the wall
  • Material: vinyl, steel, galvanized steel or concrete
  • Soil conditions and required driving depth
  • Access for equipment, including barge work
  • Anchoring, drainage and cap details
  • Removal of an old wall and site restoration
  • Permit requirements and timing
  • Whether docks, hoists, decks or concrete need to be protected or replaced

If a quote is far below others, ask what has been left out: drainage, anchors and cap are common items.

Common Seawall Mistakes

  • Ignoring drainage. Water pressure is a top cause of wall movement.
  • Filling holes without finding the cause. Soil disappears for a reason.
  • Loading the edge. Heavy vehicles, stockpiles and structures near the wall add pressure.
  • Attaching a dock or hoist to a weak wall. Check the wall first.
  • Starting regulated work without a permit. It can lead to delays and problems when selling.

Keeping a Seawall Healthy

Walk the wall twice a year, keep drainage clear, repair small cracks and open seams promptly, and keep heavy loads back from the edge. See the seasonal maintenance checklist and consider a professional inspection every few years or after major storms or high-water events.

Frequently Asked Questions

How Long Does a Seawall Last?

It depends on the material, the soil, water conditions, maintenance and how well the wall is drained and anchored. Rather than counting years, look at the condition: leaning, gaps, sinking soil and cap damage are better indicators than age.

Can I Repair Only Part of a Seawall?

Sometimes. Localized seam, cap or drainage problems can be repaired. If the wall is moving or the anchors have failed, a partial repair may only delay the problem.

Do I Need a Permit for a Seawall in Michigan?

Usually. Work on Lake St. Clair and other Great Lakes waters generally needs an EGLE permit and often a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers permit. Requirements depend on your site, so confirm with EGLE.
