How to Choose a Boat Hoist: 4-Point, 8-Point, Elevator, PWC and Boat House Lifts

Compare 4-point, 8-point, elevator, PWC and boat house hoists by boat length, hull support and dock layout to pick the right lift.

A boat hoist keeps your boat out of the water, which reduces wear, slows algae growth and makes access easy. But hoists are not one-size-fits-all. Here is how the main types compare, based on the size guidance we use.

Key Takeaways

  • Boat length is the starting point: PWC lifts under 15 ft, 4-point up to about 25 ft, elevator up to about 30 ft, 8-point for 30 to 40 ft, and boat house hoists for boats up to 35 to 40 ft.
  • Weight, beam and hull shape matter as much as length.
  • Your dock layout and water depth affect which lift works.
  • Choose a lift with capacity to spare, and keep it maintained.

Lift TypeTypical BoatGood to Know
PWC liftJet skis, WaveRunners, small watercraft under 15 ftLightweight and easy to operate; keeps the craft out of the water
4-point liftBoats up to about 25 ft: runabouts, center consoles, fishing boatsSupports the hull at four contact points; compact, economical, simple to operate
Elevator liftBoats up to about 30 ftVertical lifting, often paired with a dock platform; suits tight waterfront areas and frequent launching
8-point liftBoats 30 to 40 ft: larger cruisers and pontoon boatsEight contact points; reduces hull stress, adds stability, suits wider beams
Boat house hoistBoats up to 35 to 40 ftBuilt into a covered boat house; protects from weather and corrosion; good for seasonal or long-term storage

Davits are another option for smaller craft. Ask us if they fit your setup.

Beyond Length: What Else to Consider

Weight and Beam

Length is only the starting point. A wide-beam pontoon boat or a heavy cruiser may need a different lift than a light runabout of the same length. Use your boat’s actual loaded weight, including fuel, gear and accessories, and pick a lift with capacity to spare.

Hull Shape

The lift has to support the hull where it is strong. Ask the contractor how the bunks or slings will contact your specific hull.

Dock Layout and Water Depth

The dock or structure carrying the lift has to handle its weight. Water depth and the bottom conditions affect installation. If your slip has silted in, see dredging 101.

Power and Controls

Most lifts are motor-driven. Think about where power will come from and how the lift will be operated.

Seasonal Use

Some owners leave lifts in place year-round; others plan to raise or remove them for winter. Read how Michigan winters affect docks and seawalls.

Capacity and Margin

A hoist’s rated capacity has to exceed your boat’s real loaded weight, not just the listed dry weight. Add fuel, water, gear, electronics and any accessories. Then leave margin. If you are between sizes, ask about the next size up. Lifts working near capacity wear faster.

Elevator Lifts in Tight Spaces

Elevator lifts use vertical movement to raise and lower the boat and are often paired with a dock platform. They are a good fit where dock space is limited or waterfront areas are tight, and for boats that need frequent launching.

Boat House Hoists

A boat house hoist is built into a covered structure, so the boat sits out of the water and protected from weather and corrosion. This is a strong choice for seasonal or long-term storage. It also involves a larger structure, so plan for structure, permits and cost.

PWC Lifts and Davits

PWC lifts are lightweight and easy to operate, and they keep jet skis and WaveRunners out of the water, reducing wear and algae. Davits are an option for smaller craft in some setups.

Before Installation

  • Confirm the dock, pilings or structure can carry the lift.
  • Check water depth at the lift location and the boat’s draft. See dredging 101.
  • Ask about permits. Boat lifts on Great Lakes waters are among the structures that generally need permits. See the permit guide.
  • Plan power and controls.

Common Mistakes

  • Choosing by length alone
  • Ignoring the weight of gear and fuel
  • Installing on a dock that was not designed for it
  • Skipping regular maintenance. See the maintenance checklist.

Frequently Asked Questions

What Is the Difference Between a 4-Point and an 8-Point Lift?

A 4-point lift supports the hull at four contact points and suits smaller boats. An 8-point lift spreads the load over eight points, which reduces hull stress and adds stability for larger boats and wider beams.

Can I Install a Hoist on My Existing Dock?

Sometimes. The dock has to be designed for the lift’s weight and forces. Have a contractor review it first.
