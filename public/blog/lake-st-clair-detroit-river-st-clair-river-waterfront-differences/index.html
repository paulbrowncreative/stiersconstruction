Lake St. Clair, the Detroit River and the St. Clair River: How Your Waterway Changes Your Seawall and Dock

Shallow bays, river current, shipping traffic and ice change how walls, docks and pilings should be built. Compare Metro Detroit’s waterways.

Metro Detroit’s waterfront is not one environment. A canal lot in St. Clair Shores, a riverfront home in Marine City and an island property on Grosse Ile are all “on the water,” yet each faces different depth, current, ship traffic and ice. Good design starts with knowing which waterway you are on.

Key Takeaways

  • Lake St. Clair is shallow; its northern arm, Anchor Bay, is only about 1 to 11 feet deep across most of its area.
  • The St. Clair River runs at roughly 1.3 to 2.1 mph between Algonac and Marysville, according to NOAA, and faster near the Blue Water Bridge.
  • The Detroit River is a busy shipping channel with islands, channels and canals of its own.
  • Permit rules differ by waterway, so confirm with EGLE which part of state law applies to your site.

Lake St. Clair and Anchor Bay

Lake St. Clair is shallow, which makes it choppy in wind and prone to silt where water is calm. Anchor Bay, its northern arm, covers more than 90 square miles yet is only about 1 to 11 feet deep across most of its area. Near St. Clair Shores, Harrison Township and New Baltimore, that shows up as boat wells that lose depth, docks that run out to reach water and hoists that must clear the bottom. On open lakefront such as Grosse Pointe Shores and Grosse Pointe Farms, wall exposure and ice are the bigger concerns. See Great Lakes water levels for how high and low water play out.

The St. Clair River

NOAA’s Coast Pilot lists St. Clair River current at about 1.3 to 2.1 mph at Algonac, Marine City, St. Clair and Marysville, depending on flow, with a rapids section around the Blue Water Bridge at Port Huron. Ships pass close to shore, and river ice is a winter issue. In Algonac, Marine City, St. Clair, Marysville and Port Huron, walls and pilings have to resist steady flow and scour as well as waves and ice, which is why anchoring, drainage and corrosion protection get extra attention. Read wood vs. steel pilings and marine steel corrosion.

The Detroit River

The Detroit River is a busy commercial shipping channel. Grosse Ile, the largest island in the river, is divided by canals and sits between two river channels, and it lies at the center of the Detroit River International Wildlife Refuge, so current, wakes, silt and ice all matter for island and canal lots.

Permits Depend on Your Waterway

Work below the ordinary high-water mark on the Great Lakes, including Lake St. Clair, generally needs an EGLE permit and often an Army Corps permit. Rivers and connecting channels can fall under different parts of state law, and EGLE district staff can tell you which applies. Our permit guide explains the process.

Choosing Structures for Your Waterway

  • Shallow lake or bay: plan for silt and depth. See dredging 101 and floating vs. fixed docks.
  • River with current: prioritize anchoring, scour resistance and protected steel.
  • Canal or island lot: plan access, drainage and boat well depth.
  • Anywhere with ice: inspect in fall and spring. See Michigan winter damage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does My Waterway Change What Seawall I Need?

Often, yes. Depth, current, ship traffic, ice and access all affect wall design and material. That is why we look at your specific frontage before recommending anything.

Which Permits Apply on the St. Clair or Detroit River?

Work in or over the water generally involves EGLE and the Army Corps of Engineers, and EGLE district staff can tell you which part of state law applies to your site.
