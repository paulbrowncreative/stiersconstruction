Great Lakes Water Levels and What They Mean for Your Waterfront Property

Record highs and later drops on Lake St. Clair show how water levels affect seawalls, docks and hoists. Learn how to plan for both extremes.

Great Lakes water levels are not steady. They rise and fall with rain, snowmelt, evaporation and long-term weather patterns. If you own property on Lake St. Clair or the Detroit River, those swings show up on your seawall, your dock and your boat lift.

Key Takeaways

  • Lake St. Clair set record high levels in 2019, and again in 2020, causing erosion and flooding.
  • By April 2022 the lake had dropped about 20 inches below the April 2020 record, showing how fast conditions can change.
  • High water stresses seawalls and floods low structures; low water exposes docks and silts up slips.
  • The Army Corps’ Detroit District publishes monitoring and forecasts.

Recent History on Lake St. Clair

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Detroit District, reported that Lake St. Clair set record monthly high levels in 2019, tied its January record in 2020 and set new monthly records through 2020. In August 2020, the lake surpassed the previous year’s record. The Corps warned that erosion and flooding were significant in many locations.

Then conditions changed. According to the Corps’ April 2022 summary, reported by local media, Lake St. Clair was about 20 inches below the April 2020 record. In roughly two years, the lake went from record highs to well below them, though still above its long-term average.

What High Water Does

  • Raises wave energy against seawalls and can overtop low walls, washing out soil behind them.
  • Floods low areas such as patios, docks and ground-level structures.
  • Shrinks freeboard: the distance between the water and the top of the dock or wall.
  • Increases erosion, especially at wall ends and unprotected areas.

What Low Water Does

  • Exposes bottom conditions and makes shallow slips and boat wells obvious. See dredging 101.
  • Raises dock height above the water, especially for fixed docks. See floating vs. fixed docks.
  • Changes how hoists and boats sit, and can make launching harder.

Designing for Both Extremes

Because levels swing both ways, good waterfront design allows for a range:

  • Set wall and dock elevations with high water in mind.
  • Choose dock types that suit variable levels.
  • Keep drainage working so water behind a wall does not build up when levels fall quickly.
  • Plan for dredging if silt is limiting depth.

Permits After High Water

EGLE reported that in 2020, shore protection permit requests were up nearly three times from five years earlier, and it worked to expedite reviews prioritized by risk. Application steps still apply. See our permit guide.

Where to Watch Water Levels

The Army Corps’ Detroit District publishes monitoring, data and forecasts for the Great Lakes on its water levels page.

Freeboard and Elevation: Why Height Matters

Freeboard is the distance between the water and the top of your wall or dock. When water is high, freeboard shrinks, and waves can wash over. When water is low, docks and lifts sit far above the surface. Elevation choices made during a low-water year can look wrong when levels rise, and the reverse is also true.

Planning Checklist for High Water

  • Inspect the seawall and cap, and repair weak spots first.
  • Check drainage so water behind the wall does not pond.
  • Look at the wall ends and areas where erosion has started.
  • Consider dock and hoist heights.
  • Know who to call and what permits may apply.

Planning Checklist for Low Water

  • Check slip and boat well depth. See dredging 101.
  • Inspect exposed pilings and wall bases.
  • Check that your hoist lowers fully clear of the bottom.
  • Look at your dock: can you still step on and off the boat safely?

Water Levels and Property Values

Buyers of waterfront property pay attention to shoreline structures, particularly after high-water years. Well-maintained walls and docks with documentation help. See the buyer’s checklist.

Why History Matters, and Why Forecasts Matter More

Past records are useful for context, but the trend to watch is the forecast. The Army Corps’ Detroit District publishes six-month forecasts and monthly summaries, which owners and contractors use to plan seasonal work.
