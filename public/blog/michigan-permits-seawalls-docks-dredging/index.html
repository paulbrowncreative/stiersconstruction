Michigan Permits for Seawalls, Docks and Dredging: A Homeowner’s Guide

A plain-English guide to EGLE and Army Corps permits for seawalls, docks, boat lifts and dredging on Lake St. Clair and other Great Lakes waters.

The permit question is the one we hear most before a waterfront project. This guide summarizes how permitting generally works for Great Lakes waters in Michigan, including Lake St. Clair. It is general information, not legal advice. Rules change, and your site may have its own requirements, so confirm details with EGLE.

Key Takeaways

  • Building docks, boat lifts or seawalls, dredging and filling below the ordinary high-water mark on Great Lakes bottomlands requires an EGLE permit.
  • Projects that need an EGLE permit on the Great Lakes also need a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers permit. One application through MiEnviro Portal reaches both.
  • Lake St. Clair’s ordinary high-water mark is 574.7 feet (1955 datum) or 575.3 feet (1985 datum).
  • Rules vary by site and project. Confirm with EGLE before work starts.

Who Regulates Waterfront Work?

  • Michigan EGLE (Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy) regulates construction on Great Lakes bottomlands under Part 325 of Michigan’s Natural Resources and Environmental Protection Act.
  • U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (Detroit District) regulates work in navigable waters. Projects on the Great Lakes that need an EGLE permit also need a Corps permit.
  • Your city or township may add its own requirements, such as zoning, building or soil-erosion rules.

What Needs a Permit?

According to EGLE, permits are required for activities such as filling, dredging and placing structures like docks, boat lifts and seawalls on Michigan’s Great Lakes bottomlands below the statutory ordinary high-water mark (OHWM). Projects above the OHWM may require permits under other laws, and EGLE recommends contacting the staff person for your county if you are not sure.

The Ordinary High-Water Mark on Lake St. Clair

EGLE lists Lake St. Clair’s OHWM at 574.7 feet under the 1955 datum and 575.3 feet under the 1985 datum. The statutory OHWM is a set elevation and may not match the natural line where water meets land on your property. Your survey or site plan should identify which datum it uses. EGLE explains the OHWM here.

How Applications Work

Applications are submitted through EGLE’s MiEnviro Portal. Once submitted, the application is automatically sent to the Army Corps, so you do not fill out a second one. EGLE staff review it and visit the site to assess effects on the public trust, neighboring property owners and the environment. Every application must include:

  • A completed online form
  • Property owner authorization
  • Project location
  • Site plans and cross-sections
  • An evaluation of alternatives and the purpose of the project
  • The application processing fee

There are three categories: General Permits and Minor Project categories for common, low-impact work, and Individual Permits for larger projects. Fees depend on the project type and category. See EGLE’s Great Lakes construction permits page.

Tips to Keep Your Project Moving

  1. Start early. Permit review takes time, and you generally cannot begin regulated work before approval.
  2. Get a survey or site plan that shows the OHWM and the proposed work.
  3. Take photos of existing conditions.
  4. Ask your contractor who prepares the application and what they need from you.
  5. Keep your permit and any conditions on file, especially if you later sell the property.

Planning a Timeline

Permit review takes time, and timing varies by project, agency workload and how complete your application is. Rather than guessing, ask EGLE about current review times, and build permit time into your plans. Because Michigan’s water is frozen part of the year and contractors are busiest in spring and summer, many owners start permitting in fall or winter for spring work.

Common Permit Mistakes

  • Starting work before approval. Unpermitted work can lead to enforcement and problems when you sell.
  • Incomplete applications. Missing drawings, cross-sections or owner authorization slow things down.
  • Using the wrong datum or elevation. Make sure elevations are referenced correctly (1955 vs. 1985).
  • Assuming a neighbor’s permit covers your project. Each property and project is reviewed on its own.
  • Forgetting local approvals. City or township rules are separate from state and federal.

What Your Contractor Can Help With

Many marine contractors help prepare the technical parts of an application, such as drawings and project descriptions. The property owner’s authorization is required, and owners remain responsible for the application. Ask who does what before you sign.

After You Get the Permit

  • Read all conditions. They can affect timing, methods and materials.
  • Keep the permit with your property records.
  • Make sure the contractor follows the approved plans. Changes may need approval.
  • Keep it if you sell. Buyers and their agents may ask. See buying a waterfront home.

Local Approvals

Above the waterline, city and township rules for building permits, zoning setbacks and soil erosion may apply to decks, patios, retaining walls and demolition. Check with your local building department early. See our deck guide for related footing and code notes.

When You Are Unsure

Contact EGLE staff for your county. EGLE recommends contacting them any time you plan work on the beach or in the water of the Great Lakes. And if you have questions about how a permit might affect your project timeline, contact Stier’s Construction with your address and photos.

Talk to EGLE and Your Contractor

EGLE staff can tell you whether your project needs a permit. Stier’s Construction can talk through what is typical for seawalls, docks and dredging on your shoreline. See our service pages for seawalls, docks and dredging, or contact us.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I Need a Permit to Replace an Existing Seawall?

Usually, if the work is at or below the OHWM on Great Lakes bottomlands. Some like-for-like work may qualify for simpler categories. Ask EGLE about your site.

Does the Army Corps Need a Separate Application?

No. According to EGLE, once you submit through MiEnviro Portal the application is automatically sent to the Corps.

What If My Project Is Above the Ordinary High-Water Mark?

EGLE’s Part 325 permit may not apply, but other state laws or local rules may. Contact EGLE’s staff for your county and your local building department.
