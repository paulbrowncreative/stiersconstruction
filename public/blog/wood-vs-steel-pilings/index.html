Wood vs. Steel Pilings: How to Choose for Docks, Seawalls and Piers

Learn what pilings do, how wood and steel compare, why piling covers matter and what to expect when pilings are installed or removed.

Pilings are the hidden workhorses of waterfront construction. They hold up docks, anchor floating docks, support piers and boat hoists, and reinforce seawalls and retaining walls. When one fails, the structure above it does not stay put for long.

Key Takeaways

  • Pilings carry loads down to firm ground and anchor docks, piers, seawalls and retaining walls.
  • Wood is a traditional, widely used choice for docks. Steel is stronger and used where loads and conditions are demanding.
  • Piling covers protect the top of a piling and extend its life.
  • Old or damaged pilings can be pulled and replaced, one at a time or as a whole structure.

What Pilings Do

A piling is a long post driven into the bed or ground until it can carry load or resist movement. Depending on the project, pilings:

  • Support a fixed dock or pier deck.
  • Hold floating docks in position while letting them rise and fall.
  • Carry boat hoists and boat house structures.
  • Help anchor and reinforce seawalls and retaining walls.

Wood Pilings

Wood is a traditional choice for many docks, and installers know it well. Wood pilings suit many residential and lighter-duty jobs. The main issues are decay and wear, especially near the waterline and at the top, and damage from ice and boats.

Steel Pilings

Steel is stronger and is used where loads and conditions are demanding: seawall and retaining-wall pilings, heavier piers, marinas and commercial work. Steel needs corrosion protection over its life, which is why coatings and inspection matter. Our post on marine steel corrosion explains the basics.

Wood vs. Steel at a Glance

FactorWoodSteel
Typical useResidential and lighter-duty docksSeawalls, retaining walls, heavier piers, marinas
StrengthSuits many dock loadsStronger for demanding loads
Main concernDecay, wear, ice and boat damageCorrosion protection
InstallationFamiliar and widely usedTypically needs heavier equipment

Piling Covers

A cover fits over the top of a piling to keep water and weather out of the most exposed part. Covers protect your investment and help extend its life, and they are a small line item compared with replacing a piling.

Custom Pile Design

Unusual loads, soft soils or demanding sites may call for custom pile design and engineering. Tell your contractor about the loads and conditions so the design fits.

Removing Old or Damaged Pilings

Pilings can be extracted, whether it is a single damaged piling or an entire structure. Careful removal limits disturbance to the surrounding area. Old pilings are sometimes removed as part of a dock replacement or dredging project.

Pilings and Permits

Pilings are typically part of the permit for the dock or seawall they support. See Michigan permits for seawalls, docks and dredging.

How Pilings Are Installed

Pilings are driven or set into the ground or bed. On the water, crews often work from a barge with an excavator and driving equipment; on shore, equipment can work from land. Vibratory hammers are common for steel piles. The method depends on the piling type, soil, access and permit conditions. Our project photos show pilings and barge work.

Spacing and Loads

How many pilings you need, and how far apart, depends on the structure and loads: the dock, any hoist, snow, people and boats. This is a design question, not a rule of thumb. If a contractor gives a piling count, ask how they arrived at it.

Signs of a Failing Piling

SignWhat It May Mean
Soft or rotted wood near the waterlineDecay in the most exposed zone
Heavy rust, flaking or holes in steelCorrosion and section loss
Leaning or shifted pilingLoss of support, ice lift or impact
Dock movement when walked onLoose connections or failing supports
Cracks or splits at the topWeathering; consider a cover

Ice and Pilings

Ice can grip pilings and lift or twist them as water levels change. Pilings that have moved over winter should be checked in spring. See how Michigan winters damage docks.

Pilings for Seawalls and Retaining Walls

Pilings are also used as part of seawall and retaining-wall systems. Steel pilings are common for these jobs. See the seawall anatomy guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

How Do I Know If a Piling Is Failing?

Look for rot or heavy corrosion near the waterline, leaning, cracks, movement when the dock is loaded and visible wear at the top. A contractor can check for problems you cannot see.

Can I Replace a Single Piling?

Often yes. A single damaged piling can be removed and replaced without rebuilding the whole structure, depending on the condition of the rest.
